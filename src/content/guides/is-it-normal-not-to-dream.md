Many people wake up and feel they did not dream at all. Some worry that something is wrong. Others notice that they used to dream often and now rarely do. This guide explains what sleep research says about dreaming and recall, and where the line lies between a normal pattern and a sign to talk with a doctor.

## Do People Dream Every Night?

Dreams are most vivid and most often remembered when people wake during REM sleep, the stage in which the brain is highly active and the eyes move quickly. Research summarized in *The Scientist* reports that people woken from REM sleep recall a dream in up to 90 percent of cases, compared with about 50 percent for awakenings in deep NREM sleep. The same article notes that people dream in all sleep stages, not only during REM.

So the honest answer is that most people probably dream every night, but the amount they remember varies widely. The key distinction is between dreaming and recalling a dream.

## Not Recalling Is Not the Same as Not Dreaming

Dream recall is measured by how often people remember their dreams, and researchers have shown that it differs greatly between individuals and also within the same person over time. Michael Schredl, a dream researcher, notes in his work that subjective recall does not always give a reliable estimate of how much a person actually dreams. A person who says they never dream may still have dreams that were not remembered.

This matters because it changes the question. "Why don't I dream?" often becomes "Why don't I remember my dreams?" Those are different problems, and they have different answers.

## What Affects How Much You Remember

Several factors have been linked to dream recall in research:

- **Awakenings.** Dream recall tends to be higher in people who wake up more often during the night. A 2019 study by Schredl in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* argues that differences in dream recall between healthy people can be explained largely by how often they are briefly aroused from NREM sleep.
- **Attitude toward dreams.** A 2017 study by Schredl and Göritz in *Dreaming* linked dream recall frequency with attitude toward dreams and with personality traits from the Big Five model. Interest in dreams often goes with better recall.
- **Stress and tiredness.** Daytime stress and tiredness have been associated with recall in non-student samples, as reported in research by Schredl and colleagues.
- **Age.** Recall patterns change over a lifetime, and the effect of daytime factors may differ with age.

If your recall has dropped, the cause may be as ordinary as a busier schedule, poorer sleep, or simply stopping the habit of noticing dreams.

## Very Rare Dreaming: What Is Known

A small number of people report almost no dream recall, and clinical reports describe people who had very few dreams over long periods. Researchers have studied such cases, but the evidence is limited and the groups are small. In one review of the research on dream and sleep, a group of adults with an absence of dreaming, confirmed through awakenings in both REM and NREM sleep, were not found to have increased sleep problems. That finding is based on a small sample and should be read cautiously.

For most people, the situation is much simpler: they dream, but they do not keep the memories.

## When to Talk With a Doctor

Not dreaming, or not remembering dreams, is usually not a medical concern on its own. It is worth talking with a doctor or sleep specialist if:

- your sleep has changed suddenly, and you wake often or feel unrested;
- you snore loudly, stop breathing during sleep, or feel very sleepy during the day;
- your recall changed after a new medication or a major life event;
- you feel worried about your sleep and it affects your daily life.

## Ways to Recall More Dreams

If you want to remember more of what happens at night, the research points to a few practical steps:

1. Keep a notebook or app by your bed, and write down anything you remember as soon as you wake, even a single image or feeling.
2. Stay still for a moment after waking, without moving or checking your phone. This helps fragments stay in memory.
3. Review your notes every few days. Patterns often show up only after a week or two of entries.
4. Stay curious about dreams. People who expect to dream and want to understand them tend to recall more.

## Questions to Reflect On

- Do you feel rested in the morning, and do you wake up during the night?
- Did your recall change recently, with stress, a new routine, or a change in sleep?
- Would you like to remember more dreams, or are you content with the way your sleep feels now?
