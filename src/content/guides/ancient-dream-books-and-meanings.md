Long before modern sleep labs and dream journals, people wrote down dreams and tried to understand them. Ancient dream books are some of the oldest surviving texts about the mind and the night. This guide looks at the most important ones, explains how they worked, and shows how they differ from the way dreams are studied today.

## Egypt: The Chester Beatty Papyrus III

The best-known Egyptian dream book is Papyrus Chester Beatty III, now in the British Museum. It dates to the Ramesside period, roughly the 13th century BCE, and was found at Deir el-Medina, the village of royal tomb workers. The text lists dream scenarios and gives a verdict for each, marking them as good or bad omens.

One feature stands out: many of the interpretations rely on wordplay. In Egyptian, a dream image could be linked to another word that sounded similar, and that sound connection carried the meaning. A dream about a donkey, for example, could be read through a word meaning promotion, because the two sounded alike. This is a clear sign that the interpretations were built on language, not on the dream's emotional content.

## Mesopotamia: Omens in Clay

Dream interpretation was also important in Mesopotamia. Assyriologist A. Leo Oppenheim's study *The Interpretation of Dreams in the Ancient Near East* (1956) remains a key work on this material. Oppenheim showed that dream reports and interpretations from Mesopotamian sources followed recurring patterns. Many were treated as omens, signs sent by the gods that predicted what would happen to the dreamer or to the king.

A well-known collection is the *Iškar Zaqīqu*, a series of dream omens that records a dream and its predicted result. Like the Egyptian texts, it reads dreams as messages about the future, and it treats the dreamer's status and the situation as part of the interpretation.

## Greece and Rome: Artemidorus of Daldis

The most complete surviving ancient dream book in Greek is the *Oneirocritica* by Artemidorus of Daldis, written in the 2nd century CE. It is in five books. The first three set out a method of interpretation, the fourth explains the technique to his son, and the fifth presents ninety-five dreams that Artemidorus collected for his son to practise on.

Artemidorus took a more systematic approach than many earlier writers. He sorted dreams by the dreamer's social position, profession, and circumstances, and he often explained interpretations by wordplay or by analogy. He still believed that dreams could predict the future. Freud later cited Artemidorus as an example of interpreting dreams like a rebus, a puzzle in which images stand for words.

## China: Traditional Dream Books

Chinese dream books, sometimes linked in tradition to the Duke of Zhou, appear in later periods and are widely known in popular culture. The attribution to the Duke of Zhou is traditional and not a reliable date for the text. These books list dream images with favourable or unfavourable outcomes, similar in structure to the Egyptian and Greek works. If you want a historical account of a specific Chinese text, consult a specialist source for its dating and transmission.

## What Ancient Dream Books Have in Common

Across these traditions, the pattern is similar:

- dreams were treated as messages, often about the future;
- the dreamer's identity and situation mattered;
- interpretations were often based on wordplay, analogy, or a fixed list of images;
- the outcome was usually stated clearly as favourable or unfavourable.

These features explain why ancient dream books feel familiar to modern readers. They look like the symbol dictionaries many people use today.

## How Modern Dream Research Differs

Modern dream research does not treat dreams as predictions. Dream researchers study dream reports and their content, and one of the main ideas is the continuity hypothesis: dreams generally reflect what matters to a person in waking life, not the future (see the Sources). A dream book that says a particular image predicts a particular outcome makes a claim that dream research does not support.

Ancient dream books remain valuable as historical records. They show what people feared, hoped for, and believed about the night. They are sources for cultural history, not guides to the future.

## Reading Ancient Dream Books Today

If you read an ancient dream book, treat it as a window into the past. Ask what the text reveals about the culture: its concerns, its language, its ideas about gods and fate, and how it sorted dreams. Those questions teach more than trying to apply the verdicts to your own life.
